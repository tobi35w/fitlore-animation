## Verified source facts

Sources:
1. I-Min Lee, MBBS, ScD; Eric J. Shiroma, ScD; Masamitsu Kamada, PhD; et al. (2019). "Association of Step Volume and Intensity With All-Cause Mortality in Older Healthcare Women." JAMA Internal Medicine, 179(8): 1105–1112. DOI: 10.1001/jamainternmed.2019.1664.
2. Amanda E. Paluch, PhD; Shreejoy J. Shrestha, PhD; Matthew B. Gabriel, MPH; et al. (2022). "Daily steps and all-cause mortality: a meta-analysis of 15 international cohorts." The Lancet Public Health, 7(3): e219–e228. DOI: 10.1016/S2468-2667(21)00302-9.
3. Catrine Tudor-Locke, David R. Bassett Jr. (2004). "How Many Steps/Day Are Enough? Preliminary Pedometer Indices for Public Health." Sports Medicine, 34(1): 1–8.
4. Historical commercial origin: In 1965, following the 1964 Tokyo Olympics, Yamasa Clock and Instrument Company marketed the world's first wearable pedometer named "Manpo-kei" (万歩計), which translates literally to "10,000-steps meter". Dr. Yoshiro Hatano proposed the target as a caloric burn heuristic, but the round figure was adopted as an advertising slogan because the Japanese character for 10,000 (万) resembles a person walking.

Core insights & cause-and-effect:
1. The 10,000 steps per day target originated as a marketing campaign in Japan in 1965, not from epidemiological health guidelines.
2. Large-scale prospective cohort studies (including Harvard's study of 16,741 women and the Lancet 15-cohort meta-analysis) demonstrate that all-cause mortality risk decreases progressively from 4,000 to approximately 7,500 steps per day, where the curve levels off.
3. Beyond 7,500–8,000 steps per day, additional mortality reductions are statistically negligible for general health.
4. Stressing or sacrificing sleep to pace indoors late at night just to reach an arbitrary 10,000-step counter is counterproductive, as sleep deprivation elevates cortisol and blunts metabolic recovery.

Everyday analogy:
Obsessing over 10,000 steps when you have already walked 7,500 is like keeping an oven preheating for an extra hour after your pie is already baked—it doesn't improve the crust, it just drains your energy.

## Spoken script · 149 words

Marcus paced circles around his coffee table at midnight, desperate for twenty-six hundred more steps.
His smart watch flashed a mocking red ring.
Seven thousand was not enough; only the holy ten thousand could save him.
He marched past his sleeping cat.
He did frantic high knees while brushing his teeth.
At midnight, Marcus stubbed his toe and collapsed onto the rug.
Luckily, his tracker registered the crash as two extra steps.
Unluckily, the entire target was invented by a clock salesman.
You see, ten thousand was never a medical prescription.
In nineteen sixty-five, a Japanese company launched the Manpo-kei pedometer.
They chose ten thousand because the Japanese character looks like a walking person.
It was an advertising gimmick, not biology.
Harvard studies show longevity benefits level off around seventy-five hundred steps.
Pushing past that doesn't add years; it just steals your sleep.
So Marcus silenced his watch, smiled at his cat, and took four steps into bed.

## Verified source facts

Source: Phillippa Lally, Cornelia H. M. van Jaarsveld, Henry W. W. Potts and Jane Wardle, “How are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world,” European Journal of Social Psychology 40(6), 998–1009 (2010), first published online 16 July 2009. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674 . The original Wiley abstract was read directly on 2 November 2026: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ejsp.674 .

1. Ninety-six volunteers chose an eating, drinking or activity behaviour.
2. They attempted it daily in the same context for twelve weeks.
3. They reported both whether they performed it and responses to the self-report habit index each day.
4. Eighty-two participants supplied enough data for analysis; the model fitted 62 and fitted well for 39.
5. More consistent performance was associated with better model fit. Automaticity increased with repetition in a consistent context.
6. Missing one opportunity did not materially affect habit formation in this study. This is the single research insight used in the narration; it is not a claim that repeated omissions have no consequences or that every workout should be repeated daily.

Physical cause and effect in the fictional story: the blank square prompts Marcus to roll up the tracker and leave; the existing bricks remain standing despite an unfinished place, prompting his return. Everyday analogy: adding bricks to an unfinished wall. The visual gap is in the top course, with nothing unsupported above it. Ending: the brick becomes a paperweight.

# Why Perfect Streaks Are Overrated

He-MAN · FitLore idea 91 · 2 November 2026

Original illustrative fiction about Marcus treating one missed workout as a failed month, meeting a bricklayer, and returning to a familiar routine. A calendar and standing bricks make the distinction between one missed opportunity and abandoning a routine tangible. This is not a documented fitness transformation, a prescribed training schedule, or a guaranteed result.

## Verified source facts

Source: Phillippa Lally, Cornelia H. M. van Jaarsveld, Henry W. W. Potts and Jane Wardle, “How are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world,” European Journal of Social Psychology 40(6), 998–1009 (2010), first published online 16 July 2009. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674 . The original Wiley abstract was read directly on 2 November 2026: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ejsp.674 .

1. Ninety-six volunteers chose an eating, drinking or activity behaviour.
2. They attempted it daily in the same context for twelve weeks.
3. They reported both whether they performed it and responses to the self-report habit index each day.
4. Eighty-two participants supplied enough data for analysis; the model fitted 62 and fitted well for 39.
5. More consistent performance was associated with better model fit. Automaticity increased with repetition in a consistent context.
6. Missing one opportunity did not materially affect habit formation in this study. This is the single research insight used in the narration; it is not a claim that repeated omissions have no consequences or that every workout should be repeated daily.

Physical cause and effect in the fictional story: the blank square prompts Marcus to roll up the tracker and leave; the existing bricks remain standing despite an unfinished place, prompting his return. Everyday analogy: adding bricks to an unfinished wall. The visual gap is in the top course, with nothing unsupported above it. Ending: the brick becomes a paperweight.


## Spoken script · 205 words

Marcus missed one workout and declared the whole month a failure.
Every evening, he trained, coloured one square green, and admired his perfect calendar.
Soon, protecting the streak became more important than enjoying the routine.
Then a storm flooded the lane.
One square stayed blank.
Marcus rolled up the calendar and marched his shoes toward the bin.
Luckily, his neighbour was building a wall.
Unluckily, Marcus was about to explain bricks to a bricklayer.
He pointed at one empty space.
The neighbour looked at the gap, then at all the bricks still standing.
You see, habits develop through repetition in a familiar situation.
In one study of everyday habits, missing a single opportunity did not materially disrupt the process.
Think of adding bricks.
Skipping one delivery doesn't make yesterday's wall disappear.
But abandoning the building site certainly stops it getting taller.
So the next evening, Marcus put his shoes beside the same doorway and returned to his usual routine.
No heroic double session. Just the next sensible step.
A week later, his calendar still had a gap.
It also had seven new green squares.
This time, Marcus used a brick to hold the calendar flat.
Apparently, progress needed a paperweight more than a perfect record.

Marcus missed one workout and declared the whole month a failure.
Every evening, he trained, coloured one square green, and admired his perfect calendar.
Soon, protecting the streak became more important than enjoying the routine.
Then a storm flooded the lane.
One square stayed blank.
Marcus rolled up the calendar and marched his shoes toward the bin.
Luckily, his neighbour was building a wall.
Unluckily, Marcus was about to explain bricks to a bricklayer.
He pointed at one empty space.
The neighbour looked at the gap, then at all the bricks still standing.
You see, habits develop through repetition in a familiar situation.
In one study of everyday habits, missing a single opportunity did not materially disrupt the process.
Think of adding bricks.
Skipping one delivery doesn't make yesterday's wall disappear.
But abandoning the building site certainly stops it getting taller.
So the next evening, Marcus put his shoes beside the same doorway and returned to his usual routine.
No heroic double session. Just the next sensible step.
A week later, his calendar still had a gap.
It also had seven new green squares.
This time, Marcus used a brick to hold the calendar flat.
Apparently, progress needed a paperweight more than a perfect record.
